**Founder decision pulse, 2026-09-08, item 3; corrected 2026-09-08 after founder review.** The founders asked
for an expert recommendation before choosing the legal posture on **using rights-cleared photographs of real
branded sneakers in editorial content**, and instructed Claude not to accept the residual risk on their

from **Israel** with an **international audience of which the United States is the largest single share**
(30.2 % and 22.6 % of followers, `../PROJECT_STATE.md`).

**What this is and is not.** It is an *operational risk recommendation* — a calibrated assessment of what
could go wrong, how likely, how bad, and what reduces it — on which the founders can make a binary choice.
It is **not legal advice and not a guarantee of legal immunity.** Where a statement rests on the reviewer's
reading of case law rather than a text verified in this session, it says so.

## 2. Jurisdiction — the United States, Israel, and what generalises

### 2.1 United States (Lanham Act)

U.S. law is materially relevant here for two reasons: the largest single share of the audience is American,
and the brands most likely to appear are U.S. rights holders. Meta is a separate matter — it enforces its own
platform policy and IP-reporting process, which is not the application of any national trademark statute, so
a removal on Instagram is a platform outcome and settles nothing about legal liability in either direction.

- **The infringement standard.** Use of a mark that "is likely to cause confusion, or to cause mistake, or to
  deceive as to the affiliation, connection, or association of such person with another person, or as to
  the origin, sponsorship, or approval of his or her goods, services, or commercial activities by another
  person" (15 U.S.C. §1125(a)(1)(A); registered marks similarly under §1114). Showing a shoe to rank, price
  or pass on it does not, on its face, suggest the brand made, sponsored or approved the post.
- **Nominative fair use.** Referring to a product by its mark to talk about it is protected where (1) the
  product is not readily identifiable without the mark, (2) only so much of the mark is used as is
  reasonably necessary, and (3) nothing suggests sponsorship or endorsement (*New Kids on the Block v. News
  America Publishing*, 9th Cir. 1992; applied to product commentary in *Toyota Motor Sales v. Tabari*, 9th
  Cir. 2010). The doctrine is court-made and its mechanics differ by circuit (the Third Circuit treats it as
  an affirmative defence, *Century 21 v. Lending Tree*, 3d Cir. 2005); the substance — identify, use no more
  than necessary, imply no endorsement — is common ground.
- **Dilution of famous marks.** The statute excludes from dilution "any fair use, including a nominative or
  descriptive fair use … of a famous mark by another person other than as a designation of source for the
  person's own goods or services, including use in connection with … identifying and parodying,
  criticizing, or commenting upon the famous mark owner or the goods or services of the famous mark owner",
  "all forms of news reporting and news commentary", and "any noncommercial use of a mark" (15 U.S.C.
  §1125(c)(3), verified 2026-09-08).
- **The limit the recent cases drew.** *Jack Daniel's Properties v. VIP Products* (U.S. 2023) and *Vans v.
  MSCHF* (2d Cir. 2023) removed the expressive-use and "noncommercial use" shelters where the defendant used
  another's mark or trade dress **as the source identifier of its own goods** (a dog toy; a shoe for sale).
  Two consequences for this desk: never use a brand's mark as the desk's own identifier (§5 rule 2), and
  **do not rely on "noncommercial use"** — the accounts are run by a business and the exclusion is read
  narrowly. The posture rests on nominative use and the absence of confusion, not on being unmonetised.

### 2.2 Israel (Trade Marks Ordinance [New Version] 5732-1972; Commercial Torts Law 5759-1999)

The founders operate from Israel; an Israeli court is the forum a brand's Israeli counsel would use, and
Israeli law governs the founders' own conduct.

- **What infringement is.** "'Infringement' – means the use by a person not entitled thereto – (1) of a
  Registered Trademark or of a mark resembling such a trademark, in relation to goods in respect of which
  the trademark is registered, or to goods of the same description; (2) of a Registered Trademark in
  advertising goods of a class in respect of which the mark is registered, or goods of the same
  description; (3) of a well known trademark even if it is not a registered trademark, or a mark so
  resembling it that it could be misleading in relation to goods in respect of which the mark is recognized
  or in respect of goods of the same description" (§1, government English translation, verified
  2026-09-08); and (4), for a well-known registered mark used on goods not of the same description, where the
  use "could indicate a connection between the said goods and the Proprietor" and the proprietor "is liable
  to be adversely affected" (§1(4), WIPO Lex text). The exclusive right is "the right to the exclusive use of
  such trademark upon, and in every matter relating to, the goods in respect of which it is registered"
  (§46(a)), extended for well-known marks by §46A: to unregistered well-known marks "in relation to goods in
  respect of which it is well known in Israel" (§46A(a)), and for well-known registered marks "also in
  relation to goods that are not of the same description, if the use of the mark by a person that is not
  its proprietor is likely to point to a connection between the said goods and the proprietor … and the
  proprietor … is liable to be adversely affected as a result of such use" (§46A(b)).
- **The statutory saving.** "Registration under this ordinance shall not prevent any genuine use by a
  person in his own name or in the name of his business … or the use by any person of any genuine
  description of the character or the quality of his goods" (§47, "Saving of genuine use").
- **Use *as a mark* is the hinge.** Every limb of §1 is framed as use of the mark "in relation to goods" or
  "in advertising goods" — that is, as a badge of origin for goods or services. Showing a brand's shoe to
  review or rank it is use of the mark to identify *the brand's* goods, not use of it in relation to the
  desk's own goods or services. The Supreme Court's 2019 Nespresso judgment (Hendel J.) is reported as
  holding that infringement requires actual use of the mark as a trademark, that stylistic distance and
  evident parody defeat confusion, and that dilution of a well-known registered mark requires a causal
  economic harm from the use (secondary source; judgment not read in this session).
- **Nominative ("true") use.** The Supreme Court in *Toto Zahav Subscribers Club v. Council for the
  Regulation of Sports Betting* (CA 3559/02, 2004) is understood to have recognised a "true use" exception
  along the lines of the U.S. nominative-use test — use necessary to identify the proprietor's goods, no
  more than necessary, and without suggesting endorsement. **Reviewer's understanding of the case law, not
  verified from the judgment text here**; the founders should have Israeli counsel confirm it if they want
  the Israeli position nailed down rather than reasonably inferred.
- **Passing off.** Section 1 of the Commercial Torts Law makes it a tort to cause one's goods or services to
  be taken for another's; it requires the plaintiff's goodwill and a reasonable likelihood of misleading as
  to source or connection (secondary sources). An editorial post that names a product to rank it does not
  represent the desk's service as the brand's. The residual exposure is the same one as under trademark
  law: a post that *looks* like the brand's own advertising.

### 2.3 What generalises, and what does not

| Generalises across both systems | Does not generalise — treat as jurisdiction-specific |
|---|---|
| Infringement turns on using the mark in a way that creates confusion or a false connection as to source, sponsorship or affiliation | The exact shape of the referential-use defence: a court-made three-factor test with circuit variation in the U.S.; a statutory "genuine description" saving plus Supreme Court case law in Israel |
| Referring to a product by its mark to identify it, using no more than needed and implying no endorsement, is the recognised safe posture in both | Anti-dilution protection for well-known marks: statutory exclusions for commentary and news in the U.S.; a "connection plus adverse effect" test in Israel with no express commentary carve-out — the exposure that most rewards keeping every piece obviously editorial |
| Neither system treats an unmonetised account as automatically outside the law | Passing off exists in Israel as a separate statutory tort; the U.S. equivalent is folded into §1125(a) |
| Platform enforcement (Meta) applies one global process regardless of jurisdiction | Which court, which remedies and which costs a brand would pursue — Israeli counsel for an Israeli operator; a U.S. claim would need U.S. jurisdiction over the founders |

## 3. Calibrated practical risk assessment

Trademark risk does not disappear because the experiment is unmonetised. What lowers it is the posture
(nominative, editorial, non-endorsing), the cleared photographs, the six-week span and the modest reach.

| Plausible adverse outcome | Likelihood over the six weeks | Severity | Handling |
|---|---|---|---|
| A brand files a trademark report through Meta's IP process and a post is removed | **Low.** A rights holder can report any post; editorial product photography rarely draws it, and a report does not have to be well-founded for a removal to happen first | **Low.** One post; the locked exception protocol marks it `REMOVED`, excludes it and recovers the slot; an IP notice is a hard stop for investigation | Protocol; screenshot; no dispute without a founder decision |
| Repeated reports leading to feature limits or, in the extreme, account disabling under Meta's repeat-infringer practice | **Very low** — requires repetition after a first notice, which is itself a stop-and-review event | **High** (the accounts are the asset) | First notice triggers the §5 re-review; no second notice is allowed to arrive un-reviewed |
| A cease-and-desist letter from brand counsel (Israeli or U.S.) | **Low**, and it rises with any of the drivers below; it becomes **plausible** once content is sponsored or a post reads as the brand's own advertising | **Medium** — legal cost and time, removal of content, a forced posture change | Founder decision with counsel; do not reply without advice |
| A civil claim (trademark infringement, dilution or passing off) in Israel or the U.S. | **Very low** for editorial, nominative use with cleared photographs and no endorsement signals | **High** | Counsel; there is no insurance in place — a fact to weigh alongside the likelihood |
| A copyright claim on a photograph | **Very low** given the pipeline — but this is the claim type that actually recurs in this category, which is why the register is not optional | **Medium to high** (statutory damages exist in the U.S.) | Provenance register; no asset without a record |

**What pushes likelihood up.** Using brand press or advertising imagery without archived permission; logo-
centric framing or brand-styled design; "where to buy" or retailer language; @-tagging brand or retailer
accounts; series or profile elements built around a brand name; a post going viral (visibility draws
attention); any sponsorship, affiliate link or paid placement; any residual replica or "UA" adjacency (the
historical exposure, removed in Week 0). **What pushes it down.** The §5 rule, applied at QA on every piece.

**Why still ACCEPT.** With the rule applied, the remaining exposure is the one every editorial outlet in the
category carries continuously, with the photograph's copyright cleared before use. The severe outcomes are
far more likely after repetition following a warning or after a departure from the posture, and both are
controlled by procedure. The alternative (§7) is not "no risk": illustrated
silhouettes still depict protected designs, and text-only coverage still names the marks.

**What would trigger a legal re-review** (stop using product photography of the affected brand until done):
any IP notice, takedown, or contact from a brand or its counsel · any monetisation, sponsorship, affiliate
or paid placement · any request from a third party to feature a product · any new imagery route beyond those
in `assets/SOURCE_CLASS_DECISION.md` (in particular any brand's press assets beyond the archived PUMA
clearance) · any use of a third-party mark in the profile, handle or a series name · a post exceeding ten
times the running median reach that centres on one brand · a change in Meta's IP or branded-content policy
· a relevant court decision in Israel or the U.S. · operating from, or targeting, a new jurisdiction · the
decision point, before any post-experiment business model is chosen.

## 4. Is the proposed posture a reasonable operating position?

Yes. It is the standard editorial posture; it matches the founders' approved brand posture — an opinionated
desk that reviews products is the fact pattern nominative use exists for; and the experiment's locked rules
(no sponsors, no links, no paid promotion, no product sold) keep every piece on the editorial side of the
line for the duration. The departures most likely to change the analysis — a post a reader could take for
the brand's own advertisement, or content paid for by a third party — are excluded by those rules and by
§5; the full list of re-review triggers is in §3.

## 5. The nominative-use operating rule — mandatory

Editorial coverage *must* name a real product by brand and model; doing so, within these limits, is the
protected use.

1. **Identify, don't brand.** Use a brand or model name only as reasonably necessary to identify the
   subject — the item name on the frame, the caption, and up to three subject hashtags. Names are written
   as the brand writes them, in the house type; never in the brand's own typography, colours or logo form.
2. **Never as the desk's identifier.** No third-party mark or name in the handle, display name, bio, top or
   bottom bands, stamps, covers, series titles or any recurring element. The desk's own handle and engine
   label are the only source identifiers on every frame.
3. **No decorative or gratuitous marks.** No standalone logos, logo crops, wordmark graphics, brand-coloured
   frames or "branded" framing. A mark appears only as it naturally appears on the photographed product,
   and the product is shown whole.
4. **No suggestion of sponsorship, partnership, authorisation or endorsement.** No @-mentions of brand or
   retailer accounts; no collaborator or product tags; no "official", "partner", "in collaboration with",
   "sent to us", "approved" or thanks to a brand. At least once during the run a DESK NOTES Story carries
   the desk's independence line (independent · unsponsored · nothing sent to us) as its method line; the
   safeguard is the absence of endorsement signals — the line only makes it explicit.
5. **Rights-cleared photography only**, recorded per asset before layout; press assets only with archived
   clearance (currently PUMA); no brand advertising imagery.
6. **Editorial context obvious on every piece:** engine label, a verdict, a ranking or a market reading, and
   sources on the frame. Never a listing, a where-to-buy post or a promotion.
7. **Proportion.** No brand dominates the feed as though the desk were its channel; the engine mix and the
   anti-duplication rules spread subjects, and QA rejects a piece that reads as a single-brand showcase
   without an editorial reason.
8. **The illustration route stays generic:** silhouettes recognisable as a model family with colour blocking,
   never a drawn brand device.
9. **Takedown playbook.** An IP notice is a hard stop for investigation (locked). Screenshot the notice and
   the guideline named; file no counter-notice or dispute without a founder decision; a second notice on
   either account triggers a posture re-review with counsel.
10. **Re-review before any monetisation or third-party involvement** (§3 triggers).

Enforced at `operations/PRE_PUBLISH_QA.md` §A on every piece.

## 6. Sourcing routes — narrowed, none removed

- **Route F (free stock) narrowed:** only photographs in which the product is the subject in a neutral
  context; no brand advertising imagery, no store or event scenes with brand signage as the subject,
  nothing that could depict counterfeit product, no people. Licence text archived per asset; Pixabay stays
  excluded for branded products.
- **Route B (brand press assets) stays PUMA-only**, with the archived clearance in the provenance row.
- **Route S (house illustration) stays generic** — it also covers a DECLINE.
- Routes O (owned) and X (permissioned) unchanged; route P (paid stock) needs founder approval per purchase.

## 7. The alternative the founders did not take — record

This is what DECLINE would have meant, kept as the record of what ACCEPT ruled out and as the fallback if a
§3 trigger ever forces a posture change. Declining means **no photograph of a branded sneaker on either
account for the six weeks** — owned
photographs included, since they show the same marks. Operationally:

- **C2** loses product photography: 147 illustrated silhouettes instead (route S, ~5 h to build the library,
  ~3–5 min per colorway). The engine still runs at 7 items in every format, but rankings of real products
  without a photograph of any of them are weaker content, and illustrated silhouettes still depict
  protected designs, so the trade-dress residual shrinks rather than disappears.
- **C1** runs on its imagery-optional layouts; **C4** on its three imagery-free formats plus illustration;
  **Stories** likewise; **C3** is unaffected.
- The accounts would be a sneaker media presence that does not show sneakers, which reads as an
  aggregator's caution rather than an editorial desk's confidence, and cuts against the approved brand
  posture. The experiment remains runnable; its C2 read becomes a read of an illustration format rather than
  of the ranking format the engine definition describes.
